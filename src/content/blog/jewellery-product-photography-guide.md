Jewellery photography is a reflection problem before it is a lighting problem. Polished metal has no colour of its own — it shows you whatever is around it. Point a camera at a gold ring and the ring reflects the lens, your hands, the room, and the black rectangle of the camera body. Solve the reflections and the photographs become possible.

This guide covers the practical controls: what to put around the product, how to communicate true scale, and how to avoid the tricks that make a piece look bigger, brighter or more valuable than it is.

## Why jewellery behaves differently

Three properties make this category unforgiving:

1. **Metal is a mirror.** Diffused light does not illuminate a ring the way it illuminates a mug; the ring shows you the light source's shape.
2. **The product is small.** Depth of field collapses at macro distances, so either the front stone or the back of the band is sharp — never both, without focus stacking.
3. **Value is judged in details.** Clasp construction, stone setting and engraving quality are purchase criteria, not footnotes.

That combination explains why jewellery photography rewards preparation and punishes improvisation. It also means the cheapest improvement is not a better camera; it is a better light tent.

## Building a reflection-proof setup

The goal is to surround the product with a controlled environment, so every reflection shows something neutral or intentional.

**A light tent or a white enclosure.** Even a homemade one from white card works: three walls and a ceiling of white material, open at the front for the lens. The metal reflects white card instead of the room.

**A black card with a hole for the lens.** Place it in front of the opening. The lens reflects as a small black dot at worst, instead of a mirror image of the camera. Move the card to change where the dark reflection lands on the product.

**One light on the outside of the tent.** A speedlight or continuous source aimed at the tent wall, not at the product. The tent becomes the light source, which is exactly what you want: a large, soft, shaped highlight.

**Grey or black cards inside the tent.** White tents give soft, even, slightly flat results — good for e-commerce clarity. Adding black cards creates contrast edges that make faceted stones sparkle and give round bands definition.

**A matte surface.** Polished acrylic reflects the product back into itself. Use matte stone, velvet, or a brushed surface, and avoid fibres that show under macro focus.

## Metal colour: the accuracy problem

Gold, rose gold, white gold, platinum and silver are different colours, and the difference matters at the point of sale. Common errors:

- **Warm light turns white gold yellow.** Set white balance with a grey card under the actual light you are using.
- **Cool light turns yellow gold green.** Especially with LED sources with a high colour temperature.
- **Reflected environment tints the metal.** A wooden table will make a silver ring look beige. This is why the tent exists.
- **Over-editing increases saturation**, making 18k gold look like 24k.

Shoot a reference frame with the grey card and, if possible, a known metal sample. Compare the final file to the piece under neutral daylight before publishing.

## Scale: the honesty problem

A ring photographed at macro distance looks monumental. A 2 mm band can fill a frame and read as 6 mm. That is a genuine commercial risk: the customer receives something smaller than they expected, and the return reason is "not as described".

Ways to communicate scale without exaggerating:

- **Include a scale reference**: a hand, a ruler, a coin, or a supplied size guide.
- **Show the piece on a hand or a neck**, at a realistic distance, even if the hero image is a macro.
- **State dimensions in the copy** and in the image alt text where relevant.
- **Avoid forced perspective** that places a small item beside a large prop.
- **Keep the magnification consistent** across a collection so a pendant and a ring are comparable.

A useful discipline: shoot one image per product at a fixed camera distance, with the same framing, so relative sizes are visible across the collection.

## Depth of field and focus

At macro distances, depth of field can be a millimetre or two. Options, in order of effort:

1. **Stop down and add light.** A smaller aperture (higher f-number) increases depth of field; the cost is more light and a longer exposure. With continuous light and a tripod, this is the simplest fix.
2. **Focus on the hero detail.** If the stone is the value, focus there and let the band fall off. Deliberate focus is better than accidental softness.
3. **Focus stacking.** Take several frames at different focus distances and combine them. Free tools exist; the discipline is a locked tripod and identical exposure.
4. **Shoot tethered or with a large preview.** Judging critical focus on a 3-inch screen is guesswork.

Whatever you choose, check the image at 100%. Jewellery shoppers zoom more than any other category.

## Staging that helps instead of distracting

- **Earrings on a matte stand** show the drop and the fitting; flat lay hides the mechanism.
- **Necklaces arranged with a gentle curve** read as wearable; a straight line reads as inventory.
- **Rings angled to show the setting**, not standing upright in a way that hides the band.
- **Bracelets on a cylinder** show circumference and clasp.
- **Chains need contrast** — a slightly darker background makes fine links visible.
- **Velvet shows every speck.** Brush it, and use a lint roller between shots.

Props should answer a question: what size is it, how is it worn, what does the clasp look like. Anything else is decoration, and decoration dates.

## Retouching: where to stop

Jewellery retouching has a narrow honest band:

**Acceptable:** dust removal, stray hair removal, scratch removal on the *background*, unifying highlight colour, straightening a chain, focus stacking, exposure and white balance correction.

**Not acceptable:** making a stone appear larger, changing metal colour, hiding setting flaws that affect quality, adding stones, smoothing out prongs, removing a hallmark.

The line is simple: retouching may remove accidents of photography; it may not remove facts about the product.

## Where AI fits — carefully

Jewellery is the category where generative editing is most tempting and most dangerous. Metal reflections and faceted stones are exactly the structures models reconstruct plausibly and incorrectly. A generated ring can lose pavé stones, invent prongs, or turn a claw setting into a bezel.

Safe uses:

- **Background replacement** around an accurately photographed piece.
- **Removing the light tent** from the frame edges.
- **Generating a consistent set surface** across a collection.

Unsafe uses:

- **Regenerating the jewellery itself.**
- **"Enhancing" stones or metal finish.**
- **Creating a lifestyle scene where the piece is redrawn** at a different size or setting.

If a generated image is used, keep the original photograph and compare the setting at 100%. See the [AI product photography guide](/blog/ai-product-photography-ecommerce-guide) for the full review process.

## A publication checklist

- [ ] Lens reflection removed or reduced to a small dark point.
- [ ] Metal colour matches the physical piece under daylight.
- [ ] Stone count and setting shape verified against the piece.
- [ ] No stone appears larger than it is.
- [ ] Scale reference included.
- [ ] Hallmark and clasp visible somewhere in the gallery.
- [ ] Focus confirmed at 100% on the value detail.
- [ ] Background free of dust and fibres.
- [ ] Retouching limited to photographic accidents.
- [ ] Alt text describes metal, stone and view.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I stop reflections in jewellery photography?

Enclose the piece in a white light tent, cut a lens hole in a black card in front of it, and light the outside of the tent rather than the product. The metal then reflects white card and a small dark spot instead of the room and the camera.

### Why does my gold look the wrong colour?

Usually white balance or a coloured surface reflecting into the metal. Set white balance from a grey card under the same light, remove any warm-toned surfaces near the piece, and compare the result with the physical item in daylight.

### How do I photograph small jewellery sharply?

Use a tripod, stop down the aperture, add light to compensate for the longer exposure, and focus on the most valuable detail. For full sharpness across the piece, take multiple frames at different focus distances and stack them.

### Can I use AI to photograph jewellery?

You can use AI for backgrounds and scene context around a real photograph. Generating the jewellery itself usually changes the setting, the stone count or the proportions, which makes the image an inaccurate description of the product.
