A product photo that works on a product page can fail completely as an ad. The reasons are structural: ads are cropped, compressed, overlaid with text, viewed for a fraction of a second, and judged against a feed rather than a catalogue.

Shooting for paid media is a different brief. This guide covers what changes, how to prepare assets that survive every crop, and which claims a creative must never imply.

## The crop problem

Every platform takes your image and cuts it. Common placements:

| Placement | Typical ratio | What it does to a studio packshot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feed square | 1:1 | Crops the sides; product can end up off-centre |
| Feed portrait | 4:5 | Taller, more feed real estate; crops top and bottom |
| Stories / Reels / Shorts | 9:16 | Aggressive crop; text and product can collide |
| In-feed video thumbnail | 16:9 or 1:1 | Wide crop that removes vertical context |
| Display / search | 1.91:1 | Very wide; product may occupy a small area |

The practical rule: **compose for the tightest crop, then export the others.** Put the product and all essential context inside a central safe area, and treat the edges as expendable.

If a single image must work everywhere, shoot it with generous margin and design the layout so a 9:16 crop still makes sense. If you can produce several, do: the extra exports are minutes of work.

## Design for the first 0.5 seconds

On a product page, the shopper is already interested. In a feed, they are not. Three things decide whether the image gets attention:

1. **Clarity at thumbnail size.** If the product is not identifiable at 120 px wide, the creative is decorative. Test by shrinking the image and looking at it from a metre away.
2. **A single focal point.** One product, one idea. Two products compete; three disappear.
3. **Contrast against the feed.** Most of the feed is white and mid-tone. A saturated background or a strong shadow earns a look; a pale packshot on a white background blends in.

That last point is why the same image rarely works as both a packshot and an ad. The packshot is designed to be neutral; the ad is designed to interrupt.

## Text, safe zones and the platform's UI

Every placement overlays interface elements: profile name, caption, call-to-action button, mute icon, progress bar. Design with them in mind.

- **Keep key content out of the outer 10–15%** of the frame. Platforms differ; generosity is cheaper than re-editing.
- **Leave room for the caption overlay** at the bottom on Stories and Reels.
- **Do not put essential text in the bottom third** of a 9:16 frame; that is where the CTA and caption sit.
- **Use large, high-contrast type.** Text that is legible on your monitor is often illegible after compression.
- **Limit text volume.** Some platforms historically penalised text-heavy ad images; regardless of policy, dense text loses at thumbnail size.
- **Check the creative on a phone**, not a desktop preview.

## Photography choices that make ads better

- **Movement or implied movement.** A pour, a hand reaching, fabric in motion — motion stills outperform static packshots in most feeds, because they read as content rather than catalogue.
- **Human presence.** A hand, a shoulder, a partial figure. It signals scale and use without requiring a full model shoot.
- **Directional light.** Harder, more sculpted light reads better at small sizes than flat studio light.
- **A clean silhouette.** Busy backgrounds compress badly; simple shapes survive.
- **Real texture.** Close crops of material, foam, grain or weave create a tactile stop that a full-product shot cannot.
- **Before-and-after framing** — but only where you can substantiate the claim. See the claims section below.

## Claims: the part that gets advertisers in trouble

Imagery makes claims whether or not the copy does. In paid media, those claims are subject to advertising rules in every market you target.

A creative implies a claim when it shows:

- **Results** the product may not deliver — clearer skin, a tidier home, a fuller beard.
- **Ingredients** not in the product — fresh fruit beside a flavoured drink.
- **Certifications or endorsements** not held — clinical-looking white sets, lab coats, award ribbons.
- **Scale or quantity** larger than the pack provides.
- **Comparisons** against a competitor, even implied by a split frame.

For regulated categories — health, beauty, supplements, financial products — assume the image will be reviewed by someone whose job is to find these. Keep substantiation on file for anything you show as an outcome.

## AI-generated imagery in ads

Generated imagery is increasingly used in paid creative, and the rules are still settling. What holds now:

- **Do not generate the product.** The item shown must be the item sold, in the correct size, colour and packaging.
- **Generated scenes are usually acceptable** if they are clearly contextual and not presented as evidence of results.
- **Disclose where required.** Some platforms and regulators require labels for synthetic media; check the current policy for each placement.
- **Keep records.** Retain the source photograph and a note of which assets were generated, so you can answer a challenge quickly.

The failure mode to avoid is a generated "use case" that implies an outcome — a generated glowing skin close-up, an impossibly clean kitchen — attached to a product that cannot produce it. That is not a styling choice; it is an unsubstantiated claim with good lighting.

## Building a reusable creative system

Brands that produce ads efficiently do not shoot per campaign. They build a library:

1. **A hero product set** — clean packshots at high resolution, multiple angles.
2. **A texture set** — macros of the material, used as backgrounds and inserts.
3. **A human set** — hands, in-use, scale, one per product family.
4. **A scene set** — two or three environments used consistently.
5. **A crop template** — safe areas marked for 1:1, 4:5 and 9:16.

From that library, a designer can produce dozens of variants without a new shoot. The system also keeps the brand recognisable across placements, which is worth more than novelty in paid media.

## Testing without fooling yourself

Creative testing has a reputation problem because most of it is underpowered. Practical rules:

- **Change one variable at a time** — image, headline, or audience, not all three.
- **Give a test enough time and budget** to leave the noise floor; a few hours of data is not a result.
- **Measure downstream, not just clicks.** Cheap clicks that do not convert are expensive.
- **Keep a control.** Randomly rotating creative without a benchmark tells you nothing.
- **Beware seasonality.** A creative that wins in a sale week may not win in a normal one.

There is no universal "image type that converts X% better". Anyone quoting a fixed uplift across brands is selling something.

## An asset checklist before launch

- [ ] Product identifiable at 120 px wide.
- [ ] Essential content inside the central safe area.
- [ ] Exports prepared for 1:1, 4:5 and 9:16.
- [ ] Text legible after compression; no essential text in the bottom third.
- [ ] Creative reviewed on a phone.
- [ ] No implied results, ingredients or endorsements.
- [ ] Product matches the listing exactly — colour, size, packaging.
- [ ] Any generated scene labelled where required.
- [ ] Source files and generated-asset notes archived.
- [ ] Alt text written for every placement that accepts it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best product photo for ads?

One clear product, one idea, high contrast against the feed, composed so it survives a 9:16 crop. Studio packshots are usually too neutral to stop a scroll on their own; they work better as the product layer inside a bolder composition.

### Can I use the same image for a product page and an ad?

Sometimes, with different crops. The requirements diverge: a product page image is designed to be accurate and comparable, an ad image is designed to interrupt. Expect to export at least two crops even when the source is the same.

### Do I need to disclose AI-generated ad images?

Disclosure requirements vary by platform and market, and are tightening for synthetic media that could mislead. Check the current advertising policy for each placement, and always keep the original product photograph on file.

### How many ad creatives should I test?

Enough to find a direction, not so many that nothing reaches significance. A small set of genuinely different concepts — different setting, different crop, different claim — is more informative than twenty variations of the same frame.
