Apparel photography has a returns problem at its core. A shopper cannot touch the fabric, check the drape, or see how a garment sits on a body that resembles theirs. Every image is a substitute for the fitting room — and the wrong format of image makes the substitution unreliable.

There are three broad formats: flat lay, ghost mannequin, and on-model. Each answers a different question. Most stores need at least two.

## The three formats, compared

| Format | What it shows | Best for | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Flat lay** | Shape, print, surface detail | T-shirts, accessories, simple cuts | Hides fit and drape; looks flat |
| **Ghost mannequin** | Garment's 3D shape with no body | Knitwear, shirts, jackets | Difficult with soft or unstructured fabrics |
| **On-model** | Fit, drape, length, proportion | Everything, especially tailored and stretch | Cost, model casting, inconsistent body types |

The practical rule: **ghost mannequin or on-model as the primary image, flat lay for detail.** Flat lays photograph quickly and scale well, but they cannot show how a garment behaves on a body — which is the main thing a shopper is trying to predict.

## Flat lay that does not look like a rumpled pile

Flat lay is the cheapest format and the easiest to do badly. The difference is in the preparation, not the camera.

- **Steam everything.** Creases read as cheapness and misrepresent the fabric.
- **Shape the garment deliberately.** Sleeves curved, hem even, collar open. Symmetry where the product is symmetric.
- **Shoot straight down**, on a tripod or a ladder, with the camera parallel to the surface. Perspective distortion makes a hem look wider than it is.
- **Use a background with slight texture** — paper, linen, painted board. Pure white plus hard shadows looks clinical.
- **Light from two sides or use a large diffused source overhead.** Fabric texture needs a raking light to be visible, but harsh light creates confusing shadows in folds.
- **Include a scale reference** for accessories: a hand, a standard hanger, or a tape measure.

For prints and graphic tees, a flat lay plus a tight detail crop is often all the imagery needed.

## Ghost mannequin without a mannequin

The "ghost" effect — a garment with shape but no visible body — usually comes from compositing: one image of the garment on a mannequin, one of the inside, combined. There are cheaper approximations:

1. **Stuff and hang.** Fill the garment with tissue or a soft form, hang it against a white backdrop, and shoot straight on.
2. **Use a half-mannequin** and crop the neck opening out, showing the inside collar.
3. **Composite two frames** — front on a form, inside collar shot separately — and mask in an editor.

What matters is that the shape is believable. A ghost mannequin that looks like a garment with no volume is worse than a clean flat lay.

## On-model: the casting decision

On-model photography is the format shoppers trust most, and the easiest to get wrong in ways that affect returns.

- **Fit accuracy beats aspiration.** A model in a size that does not match the listed size produces a fit expectation you cannot meet.
- **Show multiple body sizes** if the range supports it. One size on one model is a single data point.
- **Consistent pose and framing across a collection.** Same background, same light, same crop, so the shopper compares garments rather than poses.
- **Include front, back and a detail.** A back view is the most commonly missing image and the most commonly requested.
- **Show movement** where the fabric matters: a mid-step frame for a skirt, an arm raised for a jacket.
- **Disclose alterations.** If a garment is pinned or clipped for the shoot, that is a misrepresentation, not a styling choice.

For a small brand, the cheapest credible on-model setup is a single model, one location with good window light, and a shot list of eight to twelve frames per collection.

## Fabric is the product

A shopper is buying the material as much as the cut. Photograph it deliberately:

- **Texture detail at close range.** Knit gauge, weave, seams, hardware, lining.
- **Raking light** to reveal surface — a low side light makes texture visible where frontal light flattens it.
- **Colour accuracy** from a grey card, checked against the physical garment. Fabric shifts colour under different light more than hard goods do.
- **Transparency** for light fabrics: shoot against a mid-tone so the shopper can judge sheerness.
- **Stretch and recovery**: a simple before-and-after of a stretched hem communicates more than copy.

Colour consistency across a collection matters more than individual perfection. If the same navy appears in five products, it must look like the same navy five times.

## The image set fashion needs

| Slot | Image | Question answered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Front, on-model or ghost | What is it? |
| 2 | Back | What does the other side look like? |
| 3 | Fabric detail | Is the material any good? |
| 4 | Fit detail — waist, cuff, hem | How does it sit? |
| 5 | Flat lay or styled look | How would I wear it? |
| 6 | Scale or size reference | How big is it really? |

Add a **size and fit note** in the copy, and consider a short clip for drape-heavy pieces. Video is often cheaper than another shoot day and answers movement questions that stills cannot.

## Sizing, representation and the fit note

Fit is the single biggest source of apparel returns, and imagery is where expectation is set. A few deliberate choices close most of the gap:

- **State the size worn** in the caption or a fit note, alongside the model's height. "Model is 173 cm and wears M" is more useful than a size chart alone.
- **Photograph more than one size** where the range supports it. Two body types on the same garment communicates drape better than one perfect fit.
- **Avoid pinning and clipping** unless you disclose it. A clipped waist makes a straight cut look tailored, and the customer will return it.
- **Show the garment on a person who represents the target customer**, not a generic catalogue model. Representation is a merchandising decision, not a diversity checkbox.
- **Include a flat measurement** — chest, waist, length — in the copy, and photograph the tape measure in one frame if the product is simple.
- **Account for fabrication shrinkage** in the listed measurements rather than in the photograph.

None of this requires a bigger crew. It requires deciding that the image is a specification document as well as a marketing asset.

## Reducing returns with images

Returns in apparel are driven by expectation gaps: colour, fit, fabric quality and length. Each gap has an image fix.

- **Colour gap** → grey card, daylight comparison, avoid saturation edits.
- **Fit gap** → on-model in the correct size, stated measurements, multiple body types.
- **Fabric gap** → macro texture, raking light, honest sheerness.
- **Length gap** → full-body framing with a stated model height, not a cropped torso.

None of this requires a bigger budget. It requires deciding that the image is a specification, not a mood board.

## Where AI fits — and the apparel-specific risk

Generated backgrounds around a real garment are the safe use: the fabric, print and fit stay untouched while the setting becomes consistent. Generated lifestyle scenes work for secondary images when the garment is not redrawn.

The danger in apparel is specific. A generative model can:

- **Change the drape**, making a stiff fabric look fluid.
- **Alter the print**, especially small repeats and logos.
- **Adjust the fit**, slimming a boxy cut or lengthening a cropped one.
- **Invent details** — pockets, seams, hardware that are not there.

Any of these turns a product photo into a false description, and in apparel the consequence is a return. If you generate, compare the garment against the physical piece at 100%, including the print repeat and the seam placement. ## A shoot-day checklist

- [ ] Every garment steamed and lint-rolled.
- [ ] Colour reference frame captured.
- [ ] Front, back and detail for every SKU.
- [ ] On-model images shot in the size that will be listed.
- [ ] Model height and garment size recorded for the fit note.
- [ ] Flat lay for print and surface detail where relevant.
- [ ] Consistent background and light across the collection.
- [ ] Sheer fabrics shot against a mid-tone.
- [ ] Alterations disclosed or avoided.
- [ ] Alt text describes garment, colour and view.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best format for fashion product photography?

On-model or ghost mannequin for the primary image, because shoppers need to see fit and drape. Flat lay is best for print detail and accessories, and is the cheapest format for a large catalogue.

### How do I photograph clothing without a mannequin?

Stuff the garment with tissue or a soft form, hang it against a plain backdrop, and shoot straight on with soft light. For a ghost-mannequin effect, shoot the inside collar separately and composite.

### How many images does a clothing product need?

At minimum front, back, a fabric detail and a fit detail. Most apparel benefits from five or six, including a styled look and a scale reference.

### Can I use AI-generated fashion images?

You can generate backgrounds and secondary scenes around a real garment photograph. You should not generate the garment itself: drape, fit, print and detail would no longer describe what the customer receives.
