Skincare is the hardest category in product photography to fake, because the product is mostly packaging and promise. A shopper buys a serum they cannot smell, feel or test. Everything they know comes from the photograph: the bottle's finish, the dropper, the label, the texture, the shade of the formula.

That makes two requirements non-negotiable. The label must be readable, and the colour must be accurate. Everything else — the marble slab, the water droplets, the eucalyptus — is optional styling.

## Start with the label

Ingredient panels, volume, directions and certifications are legal text. If the photograph makes them unreadable, the customer cannot verify what they are buying, and some markets require that information to be accessible before purchase.

Rules that hold for every skincare product:

- **Shoot front and back at full resolution**, even if the back is not the hero image. Add it to the gallery.
- **Never let a generative model redraw a label.** Text becomes plausible-looking noise. See the [AI review checklist](/blog/ai-product-photography-ecommerce-guide).
- **Avoid extreme angles.** A 45-degree hero is fine; a dramatic tilt that foreshortens the panel is not.
- **Check the smallest text at 100%**, not in a thumbnail. If you cannot read the batch number, zoom in further.
- **Light the label flat.** Curved bottles are the usual culprit: a large, soft source on the front reduces the dark band that hides text.

## Taming glass and gloss

Most skincare packaging is glossy plastic, glass, or a soft-touch tube. Each behaves differently.

**Glass** reflects the room. Two reflections are almost always wrong: the camera lens and a bright window directly behind it. Fixes:

- Put a **large white card** behind the camera so the glass reflects white rather than a dark lens.
- Use **gradient lighting** — a soft source on one side, white fill on the other — to create a controlled highlight rather than a mirrored environment.
- For clear glass, light the **background** so the liquid reads as a silhouette; for opaque glass, light the front.

**Glossy plastic** produces a hard specular highlight that can blow out a label. Move the light further away and enlarge the diffuser, or place the highlight deliberately across an empty part of the bottle.

**Soft-touch tubes** absorb light and look flat under a single source. A subtle kicker from behind adds an edge that separates the tube from the background.

**Pumps and droppers** cast hard shadows. Keep the light high and slightly to the side so the shadow falls away from the label, or lift the product on a small riser.

## Colour accuracy is a compliance issue

A serum photographed warm may read as amber; a cream photographed cool may read as grey. Neither matches the product. For skincare, colour is part of the product description.

- **Include a grey card** in the first frame of each session and use it to set white balance.
- **Compare the final image to the physical product** under neutral daylight, not on a phone screen at night.
- **Avoid heavy saturation edits.** They make a peach cream look coral and a pale green gel look neon.
- **Check across screens**, or at least across one phone and one laptop. Colour management is imperfect and shoppers use both.
- **Do not "improve" the formula colour.** If the product is beige, the image must show beige.

## Texture shots: honest and useful

Texture is the most requested secondary image in skincare and the easiest to misrepresent. A swatch on skin shows how a cream spreads; a dollop on a surface shows consistency. Both are useful. Neither should exaggerate.

- **Use a matte surface** (slate, stone, matte acrylic) so the product's gloss reads.
- **Shoot quickly.** Creams oxidise and sheen changes within minutes.
- **Light from the side** to reveal peaks and valleys. Flat frontal light makes texture invisible.
- **Keep the quantity realistic.** A giant swirl implies a generous formula that may not exist.
- **Do not add water droplets** to a product that is waterless, or glycerin sheen to a matte formula. That is a claim in visual form.
- **Skip before-and-after skin imagery** unless you have substantiation and comply with advertising rules in your market.

If the brand sells several textures — gel, cream, oil — shoot them with identical framing and lighting so shoppers can compare consistency rather than being swayed by styling.

## Building a set that does not look borrowed

Skincare styling has converged on a small set of props: marble, travertine, dried flowers, water, linen. They are popular because they photograph well, not because they are true to any particular brand.

A better approach:

1. **Choose one material story** that matches the formula. Clay and stone for mineral products; glass and steel for clinical ones; linen and wood for botanical ones.
2. **Keep the palette to three tones.** A fourth usually means a prop is auditioning.
3. **Use a single anchor surface** across the range so the collection reads as one brand.
4. **Style the cap and dropper.** A dropper lying beside the bottle tells a use story; a sealed box does not.
5. **Resist the bathroom cliché** unless the product is genuinely a bathroom product.

Natural light beside a window with a white card on the shadow side will beat most artificial setups for this category, because glass and gloss look more believable under a large, soft source.

## The image set skincare needs

| Slot | Image | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Front packshot, plain background | Identification, marketplace-friendly |
| 2 | Back panel, readable | Ingredients, volume, directions |
| 3 | Texture swatch | What the formula actually looks like |
| 4 | Scale in hand | Size expectation, pump or dropper in use |
| 5 | Set or routine shot | Shows the range and how it is used |
| 6 | Packaging detail | Airless pump, recyclable tube, seal |

For a range, the same six slots repeated with identical framing makes the collection look designed rather than accumulated.

## Sets, refills and packaging changes

Skincare sells in ranges, and ranges create photographic problems that single products do not.

**Sets.** When three products are sold together, the set image must show exactly what is included, at true relative sizes. A cleanser photographed the same size as a serum misstates the value of the bundle. Use one frame with all items, and one frame with the set arranged in use order.

**Refills.** A refill pouch next to the bottle it fills is a useful secondary image, provided it is clear which item ships. Photograph the refill as the primary product when that is what the customer receives.

**Packaging updates.** Brands change caps, labels and bottle shapes. When they do, old images become inaccurate and Amazon or marketplace listings can be flagged for product mismatch. Keep a dated archive of packshots and replace every placement — product page, ads, marketplace, email templates — in the same release.

**Sizes and volumes.** If the range includes 30 ml and 100 ml versions, photograph them side by side at least once, so shoppers can calibrate. After that, keep the framing identical across sizes; identical framing with different bottle heights communicates scale better than any copy.

## Where AI fits — and the red lines

Background replacement is the safe use: the bottle, its label and its finish stay real, while the surface and backdrop become consistent across a range. Generated environments are workable for secondary images if the scene is plausible.

The red lines are specific to this category:

- **No generated labels.** Ingredient lists, SPF values, certifications and batch codes must come from the photograph.
- **No invented claims.** A generated clinical set, dermatologist imagery or before-and-after panel is a regulatory problem, not a styling choice.
- **No altered formula colour.** If the model shifts a serum from pale gold to amber, the image no longer describes the product.
- **No fake "active" ingredients** styled in frame — a dropper of vitamin C beside a product that does not contain it.

## A pre-publication checklist

- [ ] Front and back labels legible at 100%.
- [ ] No text has been regenerated or synthesised.
- [ ] White balance set from a grey card.
- [ ] Formula colour matches the physical product under daylight.
- [ ] Reflections on glass and gloss are controlled, not accidental.
- [ ] Texture shot uses realistic quantity and no misleading sheen.
- [ ] No props implying ingredients, certifications or clinical results.
- [ ] Scale reference included.
- [ ] Alt text describes product, view and any styled setting.
- [ ] Image weight optimised for mobile.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I photograph shiny skincare bottles without reflections?

Put a large white card behind the camera so the bottle reflects white instead of the lens, use one big diffused light at the side, and add a white fill card on the opposite side. Move the light further away if the highlight still blows out the label.

### How do I photograph cream texture?

Place a small, realistic amount on a matte surface, light it from the side to reveal peaks, and shoot immediately before it settles. Avoid adding glycerin or water unless the product genuinely behaves that way.

### Can I use AI-generated skincare images?

For backgrounds and secondary lifestyle scenes, generally yes — as long as the packaging, label and formula colour are unchanged. Generating the product or its claims is a misrepresentation and often a regulatory issue.

### Do skincare images need to be on a white background?

For your own storefront, a consistent branded background can work well. For marketplaces, check the main-image rules: many require a plain, usually white, background.
