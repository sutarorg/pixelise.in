Food photography for ecommerce is a balancing act between appetite and accuracy. The image has to make a packaged product look worth eating, while showing exactly what is in the pack. Get it wrong in either direction and the result is a listing that either undersells or misleads.

This guide covers packaged food and beverages sold online — jars, boxes, bottles, snack packs — rather than restaurant photography. The constraints are different: the label is part of the product, the pack must survive shipping, and the claim on the front has legal weight.

## Two images, two jobs

A food product almost always needs both:

1. **A pack shot.** The label, the net weight, the format. This is what the shopper scans to check they are buying the right thing, and it is what marketplaces usually require for the main image.
2. **A serving suggestion.** The product as eaten or poured, styled plausibly. This is what makes the shopper hungry.

The mistake is treating the second as the first. A beautiful bowl of curry with the packet out of frame does not tell a shopper what the pack contains, and on a marketplace it may fail image requirements outright.

## Photographing the pack

Packaging is a reflective, printed surface, which makes it a lighting problem first.

- **Control reflections** with a large diffused source and white cards. Foil and metallised film are the worst offenders; a light tent is genuinely useful here.
- **Keep the front panel square to the camera.** Perspective distortion warps type and makes the pack look counterfeit.
- **Light the label evenly.** A gradient across a printed panel looks like a printing defect.
- **Show the net weight and any certification marks** at a readable size. These are purchase criteria.
- **Shoot the back panel** for ingredients and nutritional information. Many marketplaces and most customers expect it.
- **Avoid props on the pack shot.** A jar with a spoon beside it is a lifestyle image, and belongs in a different slot.

For jars and bottles, a small riser and a slightly elevated camera reduce the distortion that makes containers look like they are leaning backwards.

## Photographing the serving suggestion

This is where appetite lives, and where honesty gets tested.

**Show only what is in the pack** — or clearly separate what is not. If a spice mix is photographed with fresh herbs that are not included, say so, or place them out of focus as styling rather than as an ingredient claim.

**Keep quantities realistic.** A 100 g pack that appears to fill a family-sized pan is a misrepresentation. Shoot the amount the pack actually makes, and state the yield in the copy.

**Use real serving vessels.** A chipped enamel bowl is more plausible than a marble platter for a household snack. The vessel should signal who the product is for, not who you wish it were for.

**Leave a gap for the label.** Compositionally, the product should be in frame or immediately adjacent; a shopper should never have to guess which product the recipe belongs to.

## Freshness cues that actually photograph

Food stylists use a small set of signals. Most are cheap and honest; a few are not.

| Cue | How it is done | Honest? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Condensation | Glycerin and water spray on a chilled glass | Yes, if the product is served cold |
| Steam | Real steam from a freshly heated product, or a hidden diffuser | Yes, if the product is served hot |
| Crumb structure | A broken piece showing texture | Yes, if the pack contains pieces |
| Pour or drizzle | Product mid-pour, shutter speed high | Yes, if the product is pourable |
| Fresh garnish | Herbs, fruit, seeds in frame | **Only if disclosed or included** |
| Grill marks | Applied with a hot skewer | Risky — implies cooking the pack does not require |
| Ice cream sheen | Glycerin mist | Yes, but do not imply a scoop size the pack cannot deliver |

The garnish row is where most ecommerce food photography becomes misleading. A packet of instant noodles photographed with fresh coriander, soft-boiled egg and chilli is not showing the product; it is showing a recipe. Either disclose it ("serving suggestion; garnish not included") or leave it out.

## Lighting for appetite — and for accuracy

Two lighting styles dominate:

**Bright and clean** — large diffused source, white or pale background, minimal shadow. Works for packaged snacks, dairy, beverages, anything sold on freshness and clarity. It also matches marketplace expectations.

**Dark and moody** — single side light, deep shadows, textured surfaces. Works for coffee, chocolate, spices, oils. It creates atmosphere but can hide label detail and misrepresent colour.

Both are legitimate. What matters is consistency across a range and accuracy of colour. A deep red chilli oil photographed under warm light can read as brown; a green matcha can read as yellow-green.

Set white balance from a grey card, and compare the final image to the physical product. Food colour is a quality signal and customers notice.

## Texture, temperature and time

Food changes while you photograph it. Practical sequencing:

1. **Set the scene without the food.** Props, surfaces, lighting and focus first.
2. **Place the hero food last**, and shoot within minutes.
3. **Have a spare.** For ice cream, fresh herbs, or a poured drink, keep a second portion ready.
4. **Shoot the pack shot separately**, with no time pressure — it is a packaging photograph, not a food photograph.
5. **Clean between frames.** A single crumb on a dark surface becomes the subject at 100%.

## The image set a food product needs

| Slot | Image | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Front pack shot | Identification, marketplace compliance |
| 2 | Back panel | Ingredients, nutrition, net weight |
| 3 | Serving suggestion | What it looks like to eat |
| 4 | Texture close-up | Crumb, grain, creaminess |
| 5 | Pack in hand | Scale and format |
| 6 | Range or multipack | What is included |

## Where AI fits — and the food-specific rules

Generated backgrounds are useful: a consistent wooden table across a range of jars, a plausible counter for a beverage. Generated scenes are riskier because food imagery carries claims.

The red lines:

- **Do not generate the pack or its label.** Type distorts, and a wrong ingredient list is a legal problem.
- **Do not invent contents.** A generated image showing nuts in a nut-free product is dangerous, not just inaccurate.
- **Do not generate a portion size** larger than the pack provides.
- **Do not generate "fresh" ingredients** that are not in the product.
- **Disclose styled or synthetic scenes** in the alt text where a shopper could reasonably assume the image is the product as sold.

AI is genuinely useful for testing directions before a shoot: generate five tabletop scenes, pick the one that suits the brand, then photograph it for real. ## A packaging and publication checklist

- [ ] Front panel square to camera, type undistorted.
- [ ] Net weight and certifications readable.
- [ ] Back panel photographed for ingredients and nutrition.
- [ ] White balance set from a grey card.
- [ ] Colour compared to the physical product in daylight.
- [ ] Serving suggestion uses a realistic quantity.
- [ ] Any garnish or prop not included is disclosed.
- [ ] No implication of ingredients the product does not contain.
- [ ] Vessels and props plausible for the target buyer.
- [ ] Alt text describes product, flavour and setting.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I photograph packaged food without it looking flat?

Light the pack with a large diffused source, control reflections with white cards, and keep the camera square to the front panel. Add a serving suggestion as a separate slot so the pack shot does not have to carry appetite as well as information.

### Can I use glycerin for condensation?

Yes, for products served cold. Glycerin and water mixed and sprayed on glass produces beads that last. It becomes misleading if the product is stored at room temperature.

### Do I need to show the back of the pack?

Most marketplaces and many customers expect ingredient and nutrition information to be visible. Photograph the back panel at readable resolution and include it in the gallery even if it is not the hero image.

### Can AI generate food images for my store?

You can use AI for backgrounds and scene concepts around a real pack photograph. Generating the pack, the label or the contents risks describing a different product, which is a compliance problem rather than a styling one.
