You do not need a studio to photograph a product well. You need light that behaves, a background that does not distract, and enough repetition to make the catalogue look consistent. Most small businesses already own the expensive part — a phone with a decent camera.

This is a working setup you can assemble in an afternoon, plus the compromises it involves. It is not a substitute for professional photography when the product is complex or the brand depends on texture. It is a way to stop shipping products with photos taken on a cluttered desk.

## What you actually need

A short list, with honest notes on what the cheap version costs you.

| Item | Cheap version | What you gain by upgrading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Camera | A recent phone, shot in raw or Pro mode | More control over white balance and depth of field |
| Light | A window that faces indirect daylight | Consistent, repeatable exposure regardless of weather |
| Diffusion | Baking paper or a white shower curtain taped over the window | Softer falloff, fewer harsh specular highlights |
| Background | A large sheet of white card, curving up a wall | Cleaner sweep, less dust and creasing |
| Support | A stack of books plus a phone clamp | Stability, repeatable height and angle |
| Reflector | A sheet of white foam board | Fills shadow side without a second light |
| Editing | A free editor with curves and white balance | Faster batch processing, colour consistency |

Total spend is usually under a few thousand rupees if you already own the phone. Buy the foam board and the card; improvise the rest.

## Step one: find the light

Indirect daylight is the most flattering light you can get for free. It is soft, it wraps around the product, and it does not cast the hard shadows that make cheap photos look cheap.

What "indirect" means in practice:

- A window **without** direct sun falling through it.
- Mid-morning or mid-afternoon light, not noon.
- The product placed **beside** the window, not in front of it, lit from one side.
- A white board on the opposite side to bounce light back into the shadow.

Diffuse the window if the shadow edge is hard. Tape baking paper across the glass, or hang a sheer curtain. You are looking for a shadow with a soft edge — if you can see a sharp line where light ends, the source is too small relative to the product.

Turn the room lights off. Mixed lighting (daylight plus a warm bulb) is the single most common reason amateur product photos look yellow and muddy.

## Step two: build the backdrop

A paper sweep is the cheapest way to get a professional-looking background. Tape a large sheet of card to the wall and let it curve onto the table, so there is no corner line in the frame. Weight the front edge so it does not roll.

Choose your background deliberately:

- **White** for marketplace-compatible packshots.
- **Light warm neutral** (sand, bone) for a softer brand look that still reads clean.
- **Textured** (linen, stone, wood) for lifestyle frames, not for the primary image.

Avoid pure black unless you can control the light properly; black card shows every dust speck and fingerprint.

## Step three: set the camera

Phone cameras default to decisions you do not want. Change these before you shoot:

- **Turn off the flash.** Always. On-camera flash flattens the product and casts a hard shadow.
- **Use the main (wide) lens**, not the ultra-wide. Ultra-wide distorts edges, which makes rectangular packaging look warped.
- **Tap to focus** on the product, then lock exposure if your camera allows it.
- **Set white balance manually** to daylight, or shoot raw and correct later.
- **Use a timer or volume-button shutter** so the phone does not move when you press.
- **Shoot slightly above centre** for a natural perspective, straight-on for packaging where text must be legible.

Fix the phone position for the whole session. If the camera moves between products, the perspective shifts and the grid looks inconsistent even when each individual photo is fine.

## Step four: shoot the set

For each product, five frames cover almost every need:

1. **Front, straight on** — the packshot. Label readable, product centred, generous margin.
2. **Three-quarter angle** — shows depth and the side panels.
3. **Detail** — the feature the buyer cares about: texture, stitching, spout, clasp.
4. **Scale** — in a hand, or beside a familiar object.
5. **In use** — a plausible context, not a styled fantasy.

Shoot more than you need and delete later. Digital frames are free; a second session is not.

## Step five: edit consistently

The goal is not to make each photo look its best in isolation. It is to make the set look like one catalogue.

- **Match white balance across the set** first. If one image is warm and the next cool, nothing else will fix the collection.
- **Set the background to a consistent value.** Pure white for marketplace images; the same warm neutral for brand images.
- **Lift shadows gently** rather than pushing exposure. Blown highlights cannot be recovered.
- **Correct colour against the product** in your hand, not against memory.
- **Sharpen lightly** and resize to your platform's target (2048 px square is a good default for Shopify; check marketplace minimums separately).
- **Export as JPEG at high quality** unless you need transparency.

Save an editing preset and reuse it. Consistency comes from repetition, not talent.

## The five mistakes that give DIY away

1. **Hard shadows.** A small light source close to the product produces a sharp-edged shadow. Diffuse, or move the source further away and add a bounce.
2. **A grey, dirty background.** If the white is not white, the whole image reads as a snapshot. Clean the card, re-shoot, and correct the background in post.
3. **Mixed colour temperature.** Window light plus a ceiling bulb produces two shadows in two colours.
4. **Inconsistent framing.** Products at different distances look like different sizes, which confuses shoppers comparing variants.
5. **Over-editing.** Heavy clarity and saturation make textures look artificial and colours inaccurate. Small corrections, applied to every image.

## When DIY stops being enough

Be honest about the ceiling. DIY works best for:

- Products with simple geometry and matte surfaces.
- Catalogues under roughly a hundred SKUs, where a weekend per quarter is enough.
- Early-stage stores testing whether a product sells.

DIY struggles with:

- **Reflective metal and glass**, where reflections must be controlled rather than diffused.
- **Transparent products**, which need a lit background behind them.
- **Apparel on a model**, which needs a person, a fit and a second pair of hands.
- **Scale-critical items**, where perspective error changes the buyer's expectation.

At that point, the choice is not "DIY or agency". It is usually "one professional session for the foundation images, then DIY or generated variants for the long tail". Our [studio versus lifestyle comparison](/blog/studio-vs-lifestyle-product-photography) covers how to split that budget.

## Where AI fits into a DIY workflow

The most reliable use is background replacement on a photo you shot yourself with the setup above. You keep the honest product pixels and lose the dusty card. Generated lifestyle scenes are useful for secondary images when the scene is plausible and the label is untouched.

The failure mode to watch for: generated text. If your product has an ingredient panel, a size chart or a certification mark, check every character at 100% before publishing. See the [AI product photography guide](/blog/ai-product-photography-ecommerce-guide) for a full review checklist.

## A one-page checklist

- [ ] Window light, diffused, no direct sun, room lights off.
- [ ] Paper sweep taped smoothly, no crease in frame.
- [ ] Phone fixed in position for the whole session.
- [ ] Flash off, main lens, focus and exposure locked.
- [ ] Five frames per product: front, angle, detail, scale, in use.
- [ ] White balance matched across the set before export.
- [ ] Background cleaned to a consistent value.
- [ ] Colour checked against the physical product.
- [ ] Files exported at platform size, named descriptively.
- [ ] Alt text written for every published image.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I take product photos with just a phone?

Yes, for most simple products. The limiting factors are light and stability rather than the camera: a phone on a fixed support, beside a diffused window, produces images that are good enough for a storefront and most marketplaces.

### What is the cheapest background for product photography?

A large sheet of white card taped in a curve from wall to table. It costs very little, produces a clean sweep with no horizon line, and can be replaced when it gets scuffed.

### How do I stop shadows in product photos?

Use a larger, closer light source (or diffuse a window), place a white board opposite the light to fill the shadow side, and keep the product away from the background so its shadow falls out of frame.

### Is DIY good enough for Amazon?

For the main image, Amazon requires a pure white background and a clean product presentation, which a paper sweep can achieve. Check the current resolution and category rules in Seller Central, and shoot extra margin so you can crop to their framing.
